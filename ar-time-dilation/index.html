Time dilation in simple words

Four steps. No equations needed, only one rule about light.

STEP 1

Light has a speed limit that never changes

Light always travels at about 300,000 km per second. Everyone who measures it gets the same answer, whether they are standing still or flying in a rocket.

STEP 2

Build a clock out of light

Put two mirrors facing each other and bounce a flash of light between them. Each trip up and back is one tick. That is the glowing dot in both clocks above.

STEP 3

Seen from Earth, the rocket's light takes a longer path

The rocket moves sideways while the light bounces, so from Earth its light draws a zig-zag. A zig-zag is longer than a straight line, and light can't speed up to cover it. So each tick takes longer.

STEP 4

So moving clocks run slow

This isn't a trick of the clock. Everything on the rocket slows down together: heartbeats, thoughts, ageing. The astronaut feels normal, but comes home younger than the people who stayed on Earth.

It really happens

The effect is tiny at everyday speeds, but we can measure it.

GPS satellitesTheir clocks would drift by about 38 microseconds a day (speed and weaker gravity combined). Uncorrected, your map position would be off by roughly 10 km after one day.
Muons from the skyThese particles should fall apart long before reaching the ground. They arrive anyway, because at 99.9% of light speed their internal clocks run slow.
Clocks on planesIn 1971 scientists flew atomic clocks around the world. When they landed, the clocks disagreed with clocks left on the ground by billionths of a second, as predicted.
Simulation scale: in the scene, light moves 3 units per second and one tick is one second on a clock at rest. Real light is about 300,000 km/s. Slow-down factor γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²).
